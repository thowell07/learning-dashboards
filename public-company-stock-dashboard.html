Public Company Stock

IPOs, trading mechanics, share structure, and how companies raise capital in public markets

Why Companies Go Public

A public company is one whose shares trade on a stock exchange, where anyone — from billion-dollar pension funds to an individual with a brokerage app — can buy a fractional ownership stake. Going public is the most consequential capital-markets decision a company can make.

The Core Idea

A company issues shares of stock — each share is a tiny slice of ownership. When a company "goes public," it sells shares to outside investors for the first time through an Initial Public Offering (IPO). After that, those shares trade on an exchange (like NYSE or NASDAQ) between buyers and sellers — the company itself usually isn't directly involved in daily trading.

The Critical Distinction

There are two different markets at play, and keeping them separate is essential to understanding how stocks work:

Primary Market: The company sells new shares directly to investors and receives the cash. This happens at the IPO and in follow-on offerings. This is how companies raise capital.

Secondary Market: Investors trade existing shares among themselves on the stock exchange. The company gets zero dollars from these transactions. When you buy Apple stock on your phone, Apple doesn't see a dime — you're buying from another investor who's selling.

Why Go Public?

🏦 Raise Capital

The most straightforward reason. An IPO can raise hundreds of millions or billions of dollars that the company uses for growth, acquisitions, R&D, or paying down debt.

💰 Liquidity for Insiders

Founders, early employees, and venture capital investors finally get a way to convert their ownership into cash. Before the IPO, their shares are essentially illiquid.

📈 Currency for Deals

Public stock can be used as "currency" to acquire other companies. Paying with stock avoids draining cash reserves and can be tax-efficient for sellers.

🏷️ Prestige & Visibility

Being listed on a major exchange signals credibility. It attracts talent (via stock options), customers, and strategic partners. It's a badge of legitimacy.

🏛️ Fiduciary Lens
Why this matters for wealth advisory: High-net-worth clients often hold concentrated positions in a single public stock — either from founding a company, executive compensation, or an inheritance. Understanding share structure and trading mechanics is essential for advising on diversification, lockup periods, insider trading rules, and estate planning strategies like GRATs and charitable remainder trusts.

Primary vs. Secondary Shares in an IPO

Most IPOs include a mix of primary shares (newly issued by the company — proceeds go to the company) and secondary shares (sold by existing shareholders — proceeds go to those individual sellers, not the company).

This distinction matters: if an IPO raises $500M but $200M comes from secondary shares, the company itself only receives $300M. The other $200M goes into the pockets of the selling insiders.

The Underwriting Spread — Where Banks Make Money

💲 Worked Example — Underwriter Fees

Suppose a company IPOs at $20/share and sells 25 million new shares.

Gross IPO Proceeds25M × $20 = $500,000,000
Underwriting spread (7%)$500M × 0.07 = $35,000,000
Net proceeds to the company$465,000,000

The lead underwriter and syndicate banks split that $35M fee. This is why IPO underwriting is one of the most lucrative business lines in investment banking.

Alternatives to a Traditional IPO

Direct Listing

No new shares

Existing shares begin trading on an exchange without underwriters allocating shares or setting a price. No new capital is raised. Used by Spotify (2018) and Coinbase (2021). Cheaper but riskier — no price support from underwriters.

SPAC Merger

Reverse merger

A "blank check" shell company IPOs first, then merges with a private company — taking it public through the back door. Popular in 2020–2021, but fell out of favor due to poor performance and SEC scrutiny.

The Bid-Ask Spread

At any given moment, there's a bid price (the highest price a buyer is willing to pay) and an ask price (the lowest price a seller will accept). The difference is the spread.

For a highly liquid stock like Apple, the spread might be $0.01. For a thinly traded small-cap stock, it could be $0.25 or more. The spread is essentially a transaction cost you pay every time you trade.

Example: If AAPL has a bid of $189.50 and an ask of $189.51, a market buy fills at $189.51, and a market sell fills at $189.50. The market maker pockets the $0.01 difference.

Who Sets the Price?

Stock prices are set purely by supply and demand in real time. If more people want to buy a stock than sell it, the price rises. If more want to sell, it falls. There's no central authority "deciding" the price — it's the aggregate outcome of millions of individual decisions.

What drives those decisions includes earnings reports, macroeconomic data, interest rates, sector trends, analyst ratings, company news, and investor sentiment. On any given day, a stock's price might move on hard data, rumor, or pure momentum.

Market Hours & After-Hours

🔔 Regular Session

9:30 AM – 4:00 PM ET

The primary trading window. Highest liquidity and tightest spreads. This is when most volume occurs.

🌙 Extended Hours

Pre-market: 4:00 AM – 9:30 AM ET
After-hours: 4:00 PM – 8:00 PM ET

Lower liquidity, wider spreads, more volatility. Often reacts to earnings releases after the close.

Settlement: T+1

When you execute a trade, it doesn't fully settle until T+1 — one business day after the trade date. During this window, the clearinghouse (DTCC) handles the behind-the-scenes transfer of cash and shares between buyer and seller. Before May 2024, settlement was T+2; the SEC shortened it to reduce counterparty risk.

Understanding the different ways shares are counted and classified is essential — these concepts show up in every earnings report, SEC filing, and valuation analysis.

Share Classes

Some companies issue multiple classes of stock with different voting rights. This lets founders maintain control even as they sell economic interest to the public.

Class A — Public Shares

Typically 1 vote per share. This is what trades on the exchange and what retail investors buy.

Class B — Founder/Insider Shares

Often 10 votes per share (sometimes 20). Held by founders and early insiders. Gives them outsized control over company decisions.

Real-World Example: Meta (Facebook)

Mark Zuckerberg holds Class B shares with 10 votes each. Despite owning roughly 13% of Meta's total economic interest, he controls approximately 61% of the voting power. Public shareholders collectively own the majority of the economic value but have minority voting control.

This dual-class structure is controversial: it protects founder vision but limits shareholder accountability. Institutional investors (pension funds, index funds) increasingly push back against it.

Dilution — When Your Slice Gets Thinner

Dilution occurs when a company issues new shares, reducing each existing shareholder's percentage of ownership. Common triggers include follow-on equity offerings (secondary offerings), employee stock option exercises, convertible debt conversions, and stock-based acquisition payments.

🏛️ Fiduciary Lens
Share structure matters for trust portfolios. When a trust holds a concentrated position in a dual-class stock, the trustee must consider whether the governance risk (limited voting power, founder entrenchment) aligns with the prudent investor standard. Dilution events also trigger review — is the trust being diluted unfairly? Does it make sense to participate in a follow-on offering?

Following the Money

This is the question everyone asks: "If I buy stock, where does my money go? And how does the company make money from the stock market?" The answer depends on whether it's the primary or secondary market.

When the Company Gets the Money

Primary Market Transactions

The company receives cash only when it issues new shares. This happens in three situations:

1. IPO: Company sells new shares for the first time. The IPO proceeds (minus underwriting fees) go directly to the company's bank account.

2. Follow-On Offering: An already-public company issues additional new shares. Raises more capital but dilutes existing shareholders. (Sometimes loosely called a "secondary offering," though the shares themselves are newly issued.)

3. At-the-Market (ATM) Offering: Company gradually sells new shares directly into the open market through a broker, rather than in a single large offering. Less disruptive but still dilutive.

When the Company Gets Nothing

Secondary Market Transactions

Once shares are out in the wild, they trade between investors. The company receives $0 from these trades — no matter how high the stock goes.

If you buy 100 shares of Tesla at $250/share, your $25,000 goes to the person who sold those shares to you — not to Tesla. Tesla already got its money when those shares were originally issued.

So why does the company care about its stock price? Several reasons: executive compensation is tied to stock price, a high price makes acquisitions cheaper (using stock as currency), it protects against hostile takeovers, and it signals market confidence to customers, partners, and employees.

How Do Companies Return Money to Shareholders?

📤 Dividends

Cash payments made directly to shareholders — typically quarterly. Paid per share out of company profits. A $1.00/share quarterly dividend means a holder of 1,000 shares receives $1,000 every quarter.

Dividend yield = Annual dividend ÷ Stock price. A stock at $100 paying $3/year has a 3% yield.

🔄 Share Buybacks

Company buys its own shares on the open market, reducing the share count. This increases each remaining share's claim on earnings (higher EPS) and assets.

Buybacks are now more common than dividends for returning capital. In 2023, S&P 500 companies spent ~$800B on buybacks vs. ~$600B on dividends.

🏛️ Fiduciary Lens
Dividend vs. growth stock allocation in trusts: Trust documents often specify income beneficiaries (who receive dividends) and remainder beneficiaries (who receive the principal upon termination). A portfolio heavy on growth stocks (which pay little/no dividends) may shortchange income beneficiaries, while an all-dividend portfolio may underperform on total return. The trustee must balance these competing interests under the Uniform Prudent Investor Act.

Nuances & Edge Cases

The standard model is clean. Reality isn't. Here's where things get complicated.

Insider Trading & Lockup Periods

▼

After an IPO, insiders (founders, executives, early investors) are typically subject to a lockup period — usually 90 to 180 days — during which they cannot sell their shares. This prevents a flood of selling that would tank the stock price.

Even after lockup expires, insiders must follow SEC Rule 10b-5 and cannot trade on material non-public information (MNPI). Most insiders use 10b5-1 plans — pre-scheduled trading plans that specify dates, amounts, and prices in advance, insulating them from insider trading accusations.

Insiders must also file Form 4 with the SEC within 2 business days of any trade, making their activity public.

The IPO Pop — Feature or Bug?

▼

It's common for stocks to surge 20–50%+ on their first day of trading. This "pop" is often celebrated in the media, but it actually represents money left on the table by the issuing company.

Example: If a company prices its IPO at $20 and the stock closes Day 1 at $30, the company raised $20/share. But the market was willing to pay $30. The $10/share difference went to the institutional investors who received IPO allocations — not to the company.

On 100 million shares, that's a $1 billion transfer of value from the company to favored investors. This is why direct listings appeal to some companies — they skip the underpricing dynamic entirely.

Stock Splits — More Shares, Same Value

▼

A stock split increases the number of shares outstanding while proportionally reducing the price per share. A 4-for-1 split turns 1 share at $400 into 4 shares at $100.

Splits create zero economic value — they're cosmetic. But they do lower the per-share price, making the stock more accessible to retail investors and eligible for price-weighted indexes like the Dow Jones Industrial Average.

Reverse splits (e.g., 1-for-10) consolidate shares to raise the price. Companies often do this to avoid being delisted from exchanges that require minimum share prices ($1 on NASDAQ).

Short Selling — Betting Against a Stock

▼

Short selling is borrowing shares from another investor, selling them immediately, and hoping to buy them back later at a lower price to pocket the difference.

The mechanics: You borrow 100 shares of XYZ at $50, sell them for $5,000. If XYZ drops to $30, you buy 100 shares for $3,000, return them to the lender, and keep the $2,000 profit. If XYZ rises to $80, you must buy back at $8,000 — a $3,000 loss.

Short selling has unlimited loss potential (there's no cap on how high a stock can go) and limited gain potential (a stock can only go to $0). This asymmetry makes it inherently riskier than going long.

The short interest ratio measures how many shares are currently sold short relative to float. A high short interest can lead to a short squeeze — as the price rises, short sellers are forced to buy shares to cover their losses, which pushes the price up further (see: GameStop in January 2021).

Payment for Order Flow (PFOF)

▼

When you trade on a zero-commission platform like Robinhood, your order often isn't going directly to the NYSE. Instead, your broker sells your order to a market maker (like Citadel Securities), who fills it. The market maker pays your broker for this order flow — hence "payment for order flow."

The market maker profits from the bid-ask spread. Your broker profits from the payment. You get free trades but may get slightly worse execution prices than you would on the exchange directly.

PFOF is controversial. Critics argue it creates a conflict of interest — your broker is incentivized to route orders to whoever pays the most, not whoever gives you the best price. The SEC has adopted rules to increase transparency around execution quality, and the regulatory landscape around PFOF continues to evolve.

Going Private Again — Delistings & Buyouts

▼

Public companies can go private again, usually through a management buyout (MBO) or a leveraged buyout (LBO) by a private equity firm.

The buyer offers a premium over the current stock price to acquire all outstanding shares. Shareholders vote to approve the deal. Once complete, the stock is delisted from the exchange, and the company is private again.

Example: Elon Musk took Twitter private in 2022, purchasing all outstanding shares at $54.20/share for ~$44 billion total. Twitter was subsequently delisted from the NYSE.

Companies may also be involuntarily delisted if they fail to meet exchange listing requirements (minimum stock price, minimum market cap, financial reporting deadlines).
