Module 1

What Is Venture Capital?

VC is a form of private equity that finances early-stage, high-growth companies in exchange for equity. Investors accept a high failure rate, betting that a few massive winners will more than compensate for many losses.

Core Idea

Venture capitalists pool money from outside investors, buy minority equity stakes in startups, then sell those stakes at a profit when the company is acquired or goes public. The entire model depends on a small number of investments returning enormous multiples — enough to offset the majority that fail or return little.

🎯
What VC Targets

Early-stage companies with high growth potential, scalable business models, and large addressable markets — typically in tech, biotech, fintech, or consumer sectors. These companies usually cannot access traditional bank loans because they lack revenue history or collateral.

💡
Why Companies Use VC

Startups lack the revenue history, assets, and cash flow that banks require. VC provides risk capital in exchange for equity, plus strategic advice, industry networks, and credibility that help startups grow faster and attract future investors.

Fund Structure & The Players

VC funds are organized as limited partnerships. Two main parties — the GP (the VC firm) and LPs (outside investors) — each play distinct roles with different rights, responsibilities, and risk exposure.

General Partners (GPs)

The VC firm itself. GPs source deals, conduct diligence, make investment decisions, sit on boards, and drive exits. They bear unlimited liability and typically contribute 1–3% of the fund's capital ("GP commit") to align incentives with LPs.

🏦
Limited Partners (LPs)

Outside investors who supply ~97–99% of capital. They have limited liability — they can only lose what they invested. Typical LPs: university endowments (Yale, Harvard), pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, family offices, and fund-of-funds vehicles.

Key Concept: Capital Commitment vs. Capital Call

LPs don't hand over all their money upfront. They commit a total amount, and the GP issues capital calls (drawdowns) in tranches as investments are made. This lets LPs keep uninvested capital deployed elsewhere until the GP is ready to use it.

Module 3

The Investment Lifecycle

From first LP commitment to final distribution, a VC fund moves through five distinct phases over roughly a decade.

1
Fundraising
The GP pitches LPs on their investment thesis, team track record, and target returns. Capital is committed progressively — a "first close" happens once a minimum threshold is met, with the fund continuing to accept commitments until "final close." First-time funds can take 18+ months; established firms may close in weeks.
Typical duration: 6–18 monthsFirst Close → Final Close
2
Deal Sourcing & Due Diligence
GPs actively source investments through networks, accelerators, inbound pitches, and proprietary research. A typical top-tier fund reviews 1,000+ deals per year and invests in just 10–20. Due diligence covers market size, team assessment, financial projections, competitive landscape, and technology risk.
Years 1–5 (investment period)~1,000 deals reviewed → 10–20 invested
3
Portfolio Management
After investing, GPs actively support portfolio companies: board participation, strategic introductions, executive recruiting, and follow-on investment decisions. Most funds reserve 40–50% of capital for follow-on rounds in their strongest performers — doubling down on what's working and protecting ownership stakes.
Years 2–8Board seatsFollow-on reservesPro-rata rights
4
Exits & Distributions
The GP converts illiquid equity into cash to return to LPs. The three main paths: IPO (company lists publicly), M&A (company is acquired by a strategic buyer or PE firm), or secondary sale (GP sells shares to another investor). Proceeds flow through a distribution waterfall — LPs receive return of capital first, then profits are split per the carry arrangement.
Years 5–10IPOM&A / AcquisitionSecondary Sale
5
Wind-Down
The fund entity winds down. Remaining positions are written off, sold in secondary markets, or distributed in-kind as stock to LPs. The GP reconciles final carried interest and settles any clawback provisions. Meanwhile, the same firm is typically already investing from Fund III or IV.

Module 4

How VC Makes Money

Two revenue streams for the GP: management fees keep the lights on, but carried interest is where real wealth is built. For LPs, everything depends on the power law of portfolio returns.

💼
Management Fees

Typically 2% per year on committed capital. On a $500M fund, that's $10M/year — covers salaries, office, travel, and operations. This is operational reimbursement, not profit. Fees typically step down to ~1.5% after the investment period ends (years 6–10).

🏆
Carried Interest ("Carry")

The GP earns 20% of all profits above the hurdle rate. On a $500M fund returning $2B in gains, the GP earns $400M in carry. This is the primary wealth-generation mechanism for VC professionals — and directly aligns GP incentives with LP outcomes.

Worked Example — $100M Fund

Setup: LPs commit $100M. GP invests in 15 startups over 5 years.

Outcomes: 8 companies → $0 (written off). 5 companies → return $40M combined (1–2× each). 2 companies → return $210M combined (15–20× each).

Total returned: $250M. Net profit over LP capital: $150M.

Distribution: LPs receive $100M (return of capital) + $120M (their 80% of profits). GP receives $30M carry (20% of $150M gains) + ~$10M in management fees over 10 years. Total GP economics: ~$40M on a fund they contributed ~$2M to.

In a typical 20-company portfolio, most of the total return comes from just 1–2 investments. This is why VCs need every deal to have massive upside potential.

Module 5

Deal Stages Explained

VC investments occur across multiple rounds as a startup matures. Each stage has a different risk profile, check size, and return expectation.

🌱
Pre-Seed
Idea stage. Founders may have only a pitch deck and a prototype — sometimes less. Investors are typically angel investors, friends and family, or pre-seed micro-funds. Highest risk; highest potential multiple.
Check: $50K–$500KValuation: $1M–$5MEquity: 10–20%
🌿
Seed Round
The company has early evidence of concept — perhaps a beta product and initial users. Dedicated seed funds and angel syndicates invest to help build the product and expand the team. Most companies never advance beyond this stage.
Check: $500K–$3MValuation: $5M–$20MEquity: 10–25%
🌳
Series A
The company has product-market fit and early revenue traction. This is where most institutional VC funds engage. Capital goes toward scaling go-to-market, growing the team, and accelerating customer acquisition. Tier-1 VCs (Sequoia, a16z, Benchmark) compete aggressively for the best Series A deals.
Check: $3M–$15MValuation: $20M–$80MEquity: 15–25%
🚀
Series B / C
The company is scaling fast and needs capital to dominate its market — hiring aggressively, expanding geographically, and increasing marketing spend. Risk is lower but so is potential upside. Growth equity firms and late-stage VCs (Tiger Global, General Atlantic) begin participating alongside traditional VC.
Check: $15M–$100M+Valuation: $80M–$1B+Equity: 10–20%
🦄
Late Stage / Pre-IPO
Company is valued at $1B+ and preparing for an IPO or large acquisition. Risk is substantially lower; returns are more modest but still attractive. Investors include crossover hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds, and late-stage PE. At this stage, many institutional public market investors begin crossing into private rounds.
Check: $100M+Valuation: $1B–$100B+Equity: 5–15%
Real Example — Airbnb's Funding Journey

2008 (Pre-Seed): Y Combinator + angels invest ~$620K at a $2.4M valuation. Founders were renting out air mattresses in their own apartment to pay rent.

2009 (Series A): Sequoia Capital leads a $7.2M round at ~$30M valuation.

2011 (Series B): Andreessen Horowitz leads a $112M round at ~$1.3B valuation.

2020 (IPO): Airbnb goes public at $68/share (~$47B market cap), closing its first day at $144/share (~$100B). Sequoia's ~$7M Series A investment was worth well over $1 billion.

Deal Mechanics & Key Terms

How VC investments are actually structured — the instruments used, investor protections built in, and the math behind ownership and returns.

InstrumentWhat It IsTypical Use
Preferred StockEquity with special rights — liquidation preference, anti-dilution. The standard institutional VC investment vehicle.Series A, B, C+

🛡️
Liquidation Preference

VCs get paid before common stockholders in any exit or liquidation. A 1× preference means VCs recover their full investment first. A 2× preference means they receive 2× their capital before anyone else. This is the single most important investor protection in any VC deal.

🔒
Anti-Dilution Protection

If the company raises a future round at a lower valuation ("down round"), anti-dilution provisions adjust the VC's conversion price to compensate. The most common form is broad-based weighted average — less punitive to founders than full ratchet anti-dilution.

📐
Pro-Rata Rights

The right to maintain ownership percentage in future rounds. If a VC owns 15% after Series A, pro-rata rights let them invest again in Series B to avoid dilution. These are critical for protecting stakes in winning companies — VCs negotiate hard for them.

🗳️
Board Seats & Governance

VCs typically negotiate a board seat with their investment. This provides oversight authority and influence over key decisions: CEO changes, major acquisitions, follow-on financing, and exit timing. Most startup boards have 3–5 members total.

Term Sheet Basics

The term sheet is a non-binding document outlining key deal terms before definitive legal agreements are drafted. Core terms: pre-money and post-money valuation, investment amount, liquidation preference, anti-dilution type, board composition, and voting rights.

The formula: Pre-money valuation + new investment = post-money valuation. If a company is valued at $20M pre-money and a VC invests $5M, post-money = $25M and the VC owns 20% ($5M ÷ $25M).

Deal Math Example

Series A setup: $18M pre-money valuation. VC invests $6M. Post-money = $24M. VC ownership = $6M ÷ $24M = 25%.

Exit scenario: Company acquired for $120M. VC has 1× liquidation preference on $6M — recovers $6M first. Remaining $114M is split pro-rata: VC's 25% = $28.5M. Total VC proceeds: $34.5M on a $6M investment = 5.75× MOIC.

Module 7

VC vs. Private Equity

Often mentioned in the same breath, but fundamentally different in strategy, deal structure, risk profile, and the types of companies they target.

Venture Capital
Early-stage, high-risk, equity-only
▸Invests in early-stage startups (pre-revenue to early revenue)
▸Takes minority stakes (10–30%) — no control
▸No leverage or debt used in deal structure
▸Bets on future growth, not current cash flow
▸Exit via IPO or acquisition (7–10 yr horizon)
▸Most investments fail; 1–2 wins define the fund
▸Fund sizes: $10M–$10B
▸Target: 3× TVPI or better
Private Equity
Mature companies, control, leverage
▸Invests in established, cash-flow-positive businesses
▸Takes majority or full control stakes (51–100%)
▸Heavy use of debt financing (leveraged buyouts)
▸Bets on operational improvements and margin expansion
▸Exit via resale or IPO (3–7 yr horizon)
▸More predictable, less binary return profile
▸Fund sizes: $500M–$100B+
▸Target: 20%+ IRR
🔄
Where They Overlap: Growth Equity

Between VC and PE sits growth equity — investing in companies past startup risk but not yet mature enough for a traditional buyout. Firms like General Atlantic, Insight Partners, and Tiger Global operate here, typically investing $25M–$500M for minority stakes in companies generating $10M–$100M+ in revenue and scaling fast.

Why This Matters in Your Work

For UHNW clients and institutional portfolios, both VC and PE appear as alternatives allocations. VC is typically a smaller slice (5–10%) due to illiquidity and binary risk. PE may represent 15–25% of a sophisticated portfolio. Both require long lock-up periods and are restricted to accredited investors or qualified purchasers — directly relevant to any fiduciary recommending or reviewing these allocations.

Module 8

VC Through a Fiduciary Lens

Key considerations when VC intersects with fiduciary obligations — particularly relevant for private bank, trust, and philanthropic contexts.

Core Fiduciary Question

VC investments are illiquid, high-risk, and binary in nature. A fiduciary must evaluate whether these characteristics are consistent with the client's investment policy statement, time horizon, liquidity needs, and documented risk tolerance.

⚖️
When Is VC Appropriate for Trust or Managed Account Clients?
  • ▸Suitability: Is the client a Qualified Purchaser ($5M+ in investments) or Accredited Investor ($1M+ net worth excluding home)? VC funds are typically restricted to QPs or Qualified Eligible Persons under the Investment Company Act.
  • ▸Liquidity: VC commitments are locked for 7–12 years. Fiduciaries must ensure sufficient liquid assets outside the VC allocation to cover trust distributions, expenses, and beneficiary needs.
  • ▸Concentration: A single VC fund is itself concentrated across a handful of startups. Due diligence must assess the GP's portfolio diversification across sectors, stages, and vintages.
  • ▸Prudent Investor Rule: Under UPIA and most state trust codes, investments are evaluated as part of the total portfolio — not in isolation. VC may be prudent within a diversified alternatives sleeve even though individual positions are high-risk.
  • ▸Conflicts of Interest: If the institution is also an LP in a fund it recommends to clients, robust disclosure and conflict management procedures are required — especially critical in the private bank context.
📋
Due Diligence Checklist
  • ✓GP track record by vintage year (DPI, TVPI, IRR)
  • ✓Investment thesis consistency across multiple funds
  • ✓Fund terms — fee percentage, carry, hurdle rate
  • ✓Team stability and key person risk provisions
  • ✓Portfolio company governance standards
  • ✓Valuation methodology — how are unrealized marks set?
⚠️
Key Risks to Flag
  • !J-curve: early IRR looks negative as fees are charged before exits occur
  • !Valuation opacity: marks are subjective until a liquidity event
  • !Vintage year risk: returns are heavily tied to the economic cycle
  • !Capital call risk: LP must have liquidity available on short notice
  • !Tax complexity: K-1s, UBTI for tax-exempt investors, multi-state filings
The J-Curve — Why VC Looks Bad Before It Looks Good

In a fund's early years, LPs see negative reported returns. Capital is being called and deployed, management fees are being charged, and portfolio companies haven't had time to mature. The IRR looks poor in Years 1–3. As companies grow and exits begin in Years 5–8+, the return line curves sharply upward — forming a "J." Fiduciaries must set this expectation clearly with clients upfront to prevent premature secondary market sales at a discount.

Practice Note — VC in Philanthropic Contexts

For foundations and donor-advised funds (relevant to Philanthropic Solutions work), VC raises additional considerations. Program-related investments (PRIs) allow foundations to make mission-aligned investments in startups — and these can count toward the 5% distribution requirement. Impact VC funds (climate tech, healthcare access, education) may qualify.

Key caution: excise tax on investment income applies differently than for pension funds, and UBTI can be triggered in certain fund structures — typically requiring a blocker corporation for tax-exempt investors.
