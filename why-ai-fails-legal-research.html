Why AI models fabricate the law

Legal research is the domain where large language models fail most visibly — inventing cases, misstating holdings, and confirming their own errors. This briefing examines the mechanics of why, the empirical record, and the verification discipline that actually works.

Part I · The empirical record

The numbers are not close

Stanford RegLab's landmark study tested general-purpose models on over 800,000 verifiable legal questions and found hallucination rates between 58% and 88% — with models hallucinating at least 75% of the time when asked about a court's core holding [1][2]. On the task of identifying the precedential relationship between two cases, most models performed no better than random guessing [2].

Purpose-built legal tools do better, but not nearly as well as marketed. Stanford's follow-up study of retrieval-augmented commercial products found hallucination rates of roughly 17% for Lexis+ AI, 33% for Westlaw AI-Assisted Research, and 43% for GPT-4 — despite vendor claims of "hallucination-free" research [3][4]. (Both vendors contested the methodology, and the tools tested date to mid-2024; treat the exact figures as a snapshot, not a verdict [5].)

Part II · Mechanisms of failure

Why law breaks these models

None of this is random. Legal research sits at the intersection of five structural weaknesses in how language models are built — and law punishes each one harder than almost any other domain.

Mechanism 01

The training objective rewards plausibility, not truth

Models are trained to predict the most statistically likely next token, not the most accurate one. There is no internal concept of truth — only likelihood [8][9]. A citation is a pattern: party names, a reporter volume, a page, a court, a year. The model has learned that pattern flawlessly, so when its actual knowledge runs out, it completes the pattern anyway. OpenAI's own 2025 research on hallucination acknowledged that the training objective itself creates the incentive to fabricate when information is missing [9].

A citation is exactly the kind of output that looks most correct when it is most invented.
Mechanism 02

Citations demand exact recall; models store compressed approximations

Model weights are a lossy compression of training data. Legal citations are high-precision, low-redundancy strings — one wrong digit in a reporter page and the cite is broken. Most cases appear rarely in training data, and hallucination rates track that scarcity: models err least on Supreme Court cases and most on district court material and less prominent jurisdictions [2][4]. The "Place Matters" study found rates of 45% for Los Angeles scenarios climbing to 61% for Sydney [4]. Law's long tail — where most real research lives — is precisely where the compression fails.

Mechanism 03

Post-training penalizes "I don't know"

Reinforcement learning from human feedback rewards confident, well-formed, helpful-sounding answers and effectively penalizes abstention [10]. Two consequences: models rarely flag their own uncertainty (Stanford found they struggle to predict their own hallucinations [1]), and they exhibit sycophancy — uncritically accepting a user's incorrect legal premise and building on it [1][4]. Ask a leading question containing a wrong assumption about the law and the model will often confirm it. In Mata v. Avianca, the attorney asked ChatGPT to verify its own fake cases, and it obligingly confirmed them [11].

Mechanism 04

Legal meaning is synthesized, not stored

Even when a model identifies the right case, extracting the holding is a different problem. A holding is not a sentence sitting in the opinion — it's an interpretation requiring the facts, the procedural posture, the ratio versus dicta distinction, and subsequent treatment, held together in context [12]. Common-law doctrine builds seriatim, precedent on precedent; legal opinions are not atomic facts, and some questions have no determinate answer at all [13]. Models trained to produce fluent summaries will produce a fluent, confident, wrong statement of the rule.

Misstated holdings from real cases are now more common — and harder to catch — than wholly invented ones [14].
Mechanism 05

Retrieval (RAG) narrows the problem but cannot close it

Commercial legal AI grounds generation in retrieved documents. It helps — but Stanford identified limits specific to law: retrieval systems rank by textual similarity, while legal relevance turns on jurisdiction, date, procedural posture, and hierarchy of authority, so the system can pull inapposite cases that merely sound on-point [12][13]. Retrieved passages can be topically relevant but factually insufficient, or conflict with each other [15]. And retrieval verifies what sources say — not how the model reasons about them [16].

▲ Why deep reasoning models don't escape this

Extended reasoning improves multi-step logic, but reasoning chains operate over the same unverified substrate. A hallucinated premise generated early in a chain gets treated as ground truth by every subsequent step — and in agentic, multi-step research workflows, errors propagate and reinforce across iterations rather than getting caught [15]. Reasoning makes the argument better; it does not make the sources real. Court filings with fabricated citations from sophisticated firms using frontier tools were still being sanctioned as of spring 2026 [7][14].

Part III · Verified practice

The verification protocol

The Stanford researchers' own conclusion: these tools offer real value "as the first step of legal research rather than the last word" [5]. ABA Formal Opinion 512 (2024) makes the corollary explicit — the duty to verify stays with the lawyer, always [7]. Here is what the evidence supports.

01

Match the tool to the task

Use citator-backed legal platforms (Lexis+ AI, Westlaw) when citation reliability and treatment analysis matter — their curated databases and KeyCite/Shepard's integration are the real advantage, not RAG architecture per se. General models with live web search can actually compete on recent developments and non-standard sources [4]. Never use a bare, offline general model as a citation source.

✓ Evidence: Vals benchmark — legal tools win on citations; ChatGPT-with-search outperformed on 5 question types [4]
02

Ground the model in the actual text

The single highest-leverage move: don't ask the model what a case says — give it the opinion and ask questions against the document. This converts an open-ended recall task (where models fail worst) into a reading-comprehension task (where they're strong). Caveat: models can still misquote provided text, so quotes need checking too [17].

"Here is the full opinion in [case]. Using only this document, state the holding, distinguish it from dicta, and give the pincite for each proposition."
03

Prompt against the failure modes

Require pincites and verbatim quotes for every proposition (fabrications rarely survive a demand for exact page references). Explicitly authorize abstention to counteract the RLHF confidence bias. And never embed your desired conclusion in the question — sycophancy means a leading prompt gets a confirming answer [1][4].

"If you are not certain a case exists or that it stands for this proposition, say so explicitly rather than guessing. Do not agree with my framing if the law is otherwise."
04

Verify every citation against the primary source

Non-negotiable, per every state bar that has issued guidance [18]: pull each cited authority in Westlaw/Lexis, confirm it exists, read enough to confirm it stands for the stated proposition, and run the citator for negative treatment. Verifying existence is not enough — the growth area in sanctions is real cases with fabricated quotes or mischaracterized holdings [14]. Document the check.

✓ Courts have sanctioned attorneys who verified existence but not substance; the Sixth Circuit imposed $15,000 per attorney in 2026 [7][14]
05

Use AI where it's structurally strong

Issue-spotting, generating search strategies, summarizing documents you provide, first drafts of analysis you will rewrite, and stress-testing arguments. Keep it away from unsupervised authority-generation for anything filed or relied upon. Treat every output as a draft from a brilliant, occasionally confabulating junior associate whose work you must sign.

06

Watch the long tail

Reliability degrades predictably: lower courts, smaller jurisdictions, local ordinances, very recent developments, and multi-jurisdiction surveys are the highest-risk zones [2][4]. Scale your skepticism accordingly — a confident answer about a municipal code deserves near-zero default trust.

Holding

Hallucination in legal research is not a bug awaiting a patch — it is the expected output of a plausibility engine applied to a domain that demands exact authority, hierarchical context, and honest uncertainty. The models will keep improving; the duty to verify will not move. The lawyers who win with these tools are the ones who treat AI as the first step of research, never the last word.
