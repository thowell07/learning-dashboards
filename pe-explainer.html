Module 1

What Is Private Equity?

Private equity (PE) is a form of investment where firms raise money from large investors, pool it into a fund, and use that fund to acquire ownership stakes in companies — typically with the goal of improving them and selling them later at a profit.

Unlike buying stock in a public company (where you're one of millions of shareholders with no control), PE firms take large, concentrated ownership positions — often a controlling stake — in private companies or take public companies private.

The Core Idea:

Buy a company → Fix, grow, or restructure it → Sell it for more than you paid. The difference, after fees and costs, is the profit.

Who Are the Players?

🏦

LP — Limited Partner

The investors who provide the capital. Think: pension funds, university endowments, sovereign wealth funds, family offices, ultra-high-net-worth individuals.

🏢

GP — General Partner

The PE firm itself. They manage the fund, source deals, execute investments, and make all decisions. Examples: Blackstone, KKR, Carlyle, Apollo.

🏭

Portfolio Company

The business being acquired and operated. Could be a manufacturer, software company, healthcare provider, restaurant chain — any industry.

🏗️

Debt Providers

Banks and credit funds that lend money to help fund acquisitions (more on this in the LBO section). They get paid back with interest before equity investors profit.

Module 2

Understanding Capital

Before understanding PE deals, you need to understand capital — the money used to finance an enterprise. Capital isn't just "money." It has structure, priority, and cost.

The Two Types of Capital

📋

Debt Capital

Borrowed money that must be repaid with interest. The lender doesn't own anything — they just have a contractual right to get paid. If the company fails, debt holders get paid first from remaining assets.

🔑

Equity Capital

Ownership money. Equity holders own a piece of the company. They have no guaranteed return — if the company thrives, they profit enormously; if it fails, they can lose everything. Equity holders are paid last.

Real-World Analogy

Your Home as a Capital Structure

If your house is worth $500K and you have a $350K mortgage:

  • Debt: $350K mortgage → The bank gets paid first if you sell. Fixed interest rate. Non-negotiable.
  • Equity: $150K → Your ownership stake. If the house appreciates to $700K, YOUR equity grew from $150K → $350K. That's the upside.

The Capital Stack

In any deal, capital is layered by risk and priority. Think of it as a waterfall — money flows down, and lower layers only receive proceeds after upper layers are paid.

Why PE Firms Use Debt (Leverage)

Using debt to amplify returns on equity is called leverage. It's the same concept as your mortgage — you use the bank's money to buy a larger asset than you could afford alone, and you keep all the upside appreciation.

Leverage Amplifies Returns — Example

Company acquired for$100M
PE firm puts in equity (30%)$30M
Debt borrowed (70%)$70M
Company sold 5 years later$160M
Debt repaid−$70M
PE firm gets back$90M
Return on $30M invested3× = 200% gain

Without leverage (all equity), that same $100M → $160M deal yields only a 60% gain on the $100M invested. Leverage turns 60% into 200%.

The Caveat:

Leverage works both ways. If the company declines in value, the debt still needs to be repaid — and equity can be wiped out entirely. This is why PE firms are selective about the businesses they buy.

Module 3

How a PE Deal Works

The Fund Lifecycle

1

Fundraising (Year 0–2)

The GP raises a blind pool fund. LPs commit capital without knowing exactly which companies will be acquired. A typical fund: $1B–$20B. LPs don't hand over cash immediately — they make a "commitment" and send money when called.

2

Investment Period (Year 1–5)

The GP sources, evaluates, and acquires companies. They "call" capital from LPs as deals close. Typically 4–8 companies per fund. Extensive due diligence: financial, legal, operational, market.

3

Hold / Value Creation (Year 2–7)

The PE firm actively manages its portfolio companies. This is where the work happens: cutting costs, growing revenue, replacing management, making add-on acquisitions, expanding markets.

4

Exit (Year 4–10)

The PE firm sells its investment and returns cash to LPs. Exit routes: IPO, sale to another company (strategic buyer), or sale to another PE firm (secondary buyout).

The LBO — Leveraged Buyout

The most iconic PE deal structure. The company's own assets and future cash flows are used as collateral to borrow most of the purchase price.

Real Example

KKR's Acquisition of RJR Nabisco (1989)

KKR acquired RJR Nabisco for $25 billion — then the largest LBO in history. KKR put in roughly $1.5B in equity; the rest was debt. The acquisition became the subject of "Barbarians at the Gate," a famous book and film. The deal exemplified how leverage could be used to take over even massive public corporations.

Value Creation Levers

PE firms make money three ways after acquiring a company:

📈

Revenue Growth

Expand into new markets, launch new products, improve sales execution, pursue strategic acquisitions (bolt-ons).

✂️

Margin Improvement

Cut unnecessary costs, renegotiate supplier contracts, consolidate facilities, reduce headcount. Improve EBITDA margin.

⚖️

Multiple Expansion

Buy at 7× EBITDA, sell at 10× EBITDA. Market conditions or improved quality of the business command a higher price multiple at exit.

Module 4

How PE Makes Money

Two Revenue Streams for the GP

🗓️

Management Fee

Typically 2% of committed capital per year. On a $1B fund, that's $20M/year — paid regardless of performance. Covers salaries, operations, deal costs. Often steps down to 1.5% in later years.

💰

Carried Interest ("Carry")

The GP's share of profits — typically 20% of gains above a minimum return threshold (the "hurdle rate," usually 8%). This is the real money. On a $500M profit, carry = $100M to the GP.

Why Carry Matters Legally:

Carried interest has long been taxed as long-term capital gains (~20%) rather than ordinary income (~37%) in the U.S. — a point of ongoing political and tax law debate. Understanding this matters if you encounter fund agreements or LP reporting in your fiduciary work.

Relevance to Your Work — Private Bank / Fiduciary

As fiduciary counsel and wealth advisor counsel, PE shows up in your world in several concrete ways:

⚖️

Trust Investments

UHNW clients may hold PE fund LP interests in irrevocable trusts. Prudent investor standards apply. Fiduciary must evaluate illiquidity, concentration risk, and fit within investment policy.

🎁

Estate Planning

PE interests (especially carried interest, partnership stakes) have unique estate planning implications — valuation discounts, transfer tax treatment, and step-up-in-basis issues on death.

🏛️

Philanthropic Solutions

Clients donating PE interests to charity or DAFs create specialized legal questions: valuation of illiquid assets, appraisal requirements, unrelated business taxable income (UBTI) for tax-exempt donees.

📄

Fund Docs & LPAs

Limited Partnership Agreements govern LP rights — capital calls, distributions, clawbacks, key man clauses, governance. These are the primary legal instruments you'd review in a fiduciary capacity.

The J-Curve Phenomenon

PE fund returns follow a predictable pattern called the J-Curve: returns are negative in early years (management fees + investments before value creation), then climb steeply as exits occur.

This is why fiduciaries must evaluate PE investments differently from liquid assets — the paper losses in years 1–3 are normal and expected, not a signal of poor performance.
