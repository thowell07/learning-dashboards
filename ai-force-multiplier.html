The Leverage Shift

AI doesn't replace skilled professionals — it compresses the time between thinking and doing. Tasks that once took hours can take minutes. First drafts that required research become instant starting points. The professionals who will outperform in the next decade aren't those who avoid AI, but those who develop judgment about when to trust it, when to push it, and when to override it entirely.

What changes when you use AI well

⚡
Speed of first draft

The blank page problem largely disappears. Analysis, summaries, correspondence, and structured documents can be generated in seconds — leaving your time for refinement and judgment.

🔭
Breadth of analysis

A well-prompted model can consider angles you'd have taken hours to research — surfacing frameworks, counterarguments, and relevant considerations faster than any individual could.

🔁
Iteration velocity

The cost of a second or third draft drops to near-zero. You can request "make this more formal," "add a counterargument," or "shorten by 40%" in seconds — enabling real-time refinement.

The right mental model: AI as a smart junior colleague

Key framing

Think of the AI as a very fast, very well-read junior colleague who works without fatigue — but has no lived experience, no access to real-time information, and occasionally confabulates with complete confidence. You'd trust them to do thorough first drafts, synthesize reading lists, and draft correspondence. You wouldn't let them file a brief or approve a wire transfer without review. The same logic applies here.

Prompt Engineering

Prompting is the primary interface between you and the model. A well-constructed prompt is not just a question — it's a specification. It defines role, task, constraints, format, and examples. The difference between a mediocre prompt and a well-engineered one can be the difference between a useless first draft and something you can actually use.

Anatomy of a high-quality prompt

Click each layer to understand its purpose:

Role / Persona
"You are an experienced legal counsel specializing in trust and estate law…"
▾
Sets the model's operating context. Activating a specific role shifts the model's priors about what vocabulary, depth of analysis, and tone are appropriate. More specific roles produce more relevant outputs than generic ones.
Task specification
"Review the following clause and identify: (1) ambiguities, (2) missing definitions, (3) enforceability risks…"
▾
The most critical element. Be explicit about what you want, structured as numbered items if there are multiple sub-tasks. Vague verbs ("review," "discuss") produce vague outputs. Specific verbs with clear deliverables ("identify," "list," "draft," "compare") produce actionable outputs.
Context / Input
"The agreement governs a Delaware trust with UHNW beneficiaries. Here is the clause: [CLAUSE TEXT]"
▾
Provide all relevant context the model needs but doesn't have. This includes background, relevant facts, the actual material to work on, and any constraints from the specific situation. Don't assume the model knows your client's circumstances — tell it.
Format / Constraints
"Respond in a numbered list. Flag each issue with severity: High / Medium / Low. Keep each item under 50 words."
▾
Specify the output format explicitly. Without format guidance, models default to flowing prose that may not be actionable. Use numbered lists for issues, tables for comparisons, specific headers for structured documents. Length constraints prevent verbose padding.
Negative constraints
"Do not provide legal advice. Do not suggest specific legal remedies. Flag where attorney review is required."
▾
Telling the model what NOT to do is as important as telling it what to do. Negative constraints prevent common failure modes: adding excessive caveats, going beyond scope, adopting the wrong tone, or including information you don't want. Use sparingly — too many negatives produce stilted output.

Core prompting techniques

Few-shot examples
Show the model 2–3 examples of the input→output pattern you want. The most reliable way to specify format and style without lengthy instructions. "Here is an example of the output format: [EXAMPLE]"
Chain of thought
Ask the model to show its reasoning before giving an answer. "Think through this step by step." Dramatically improves accuracy on multi-step problems by forcing intermediate reasoning to be explicit.
Role assignment
Give the model an explicit expert persona. "You are a senior financial analyst…" Shifts vocabulary, depth of analysis, and default assumptions toward that domain. Most effective when the role is specific, not generic.
XML/structured tags
Wrap sections of long prompts in tags like <document>, <instructions>, <examples>. Prevents the model from confusing your instructions with the material you're asking it to analyze. Especially useful for document review prompts.
Iterative refinement
Use follow-up turns to refine output rather than trying to get everything right in one shot. "Make this more formal." "Add a section on X." "Shorten by 30%." Conversation is the interface — use it.
Negative prompting
Explicitly state what to exclude, avoid, or not do. "Do not include legal advice." "Avoid jargon." "Do not summarize what I already said." Prevents predictable failure modes without lengthy positive specifications.

Prompt structure: before and after

Weak prompt

Prompt
User
Can you review this trust agreement and tell me if there are any issues? [PASTE OF 40-PAGE DOCUMENT]

Problems: No role context. "Issues" is undefined — legal? formatting? structural? No output format specified. 40 pages with no focus instruction will produce a generic summary, not an actionable analysis.

Strong prompt

Prompt
System
You are a trust and estates attorney reviewing documents for a fiduciary. Your role is to identify drafting issues, ambiguities, and missing provisions — not to provide legal advice or recommend action.
Context
This is a Delaware revocable living trust for a UHNW client with concentrated equity holdings. The trust will hold both liquid and illiquid assets. Review Article IV (Distribution Standards) only.
Task
<document>[ARTICLE IV TEXT ONLY]</document> Identify: (1) ambiguous distribution standards, (2) undefined terms requiring definition, (3) missing provisions common in UHNW contexts. Use a numbered list. Flag severity: High / Medium / Low. Max 60 words per item.

Improvements: Explicit role with scope limitation. Specific context about client type and asset profile. Focused scope (one article, not 40 pages). Structured task with enumerated sub-tasks. Output format and length constraints specified.

Common prompting mistakes

Mistake
Asking too many things at once

A prompt that asks for a summary, analysis, recommendations, and email draft will produce a diluted response for each. Break multi-task requests into sequential turns.

Mistake
Providing too little context

The model doesn't know your client, situation, or constraints. Assume it knows nothing specific about your context and provide what it needs to give relevant output.

Mistake
Accepting first output as final

First outputs should be treated as v1 drafts. The real value comes from iteration — push back, ask for refinement, request alternatives. The conversation is the workflow.

Mistake
Overloading the context window

Pasting an entire 50-page document when you only need section 3 analyzed reduces quality. Models perform better on focused inputs. Extract and provide only what's relevant.

Workflow Patterns

AI creates the most leverage when it's integrated into a deliberate workflow — not just used ad hoc for one-off tasks. Understanding the recurring patterns of how AI fits into professional work helps you identify where to apply it systematically, and where human judgment remains essential.

Draft → Refine

The most universally applicable pattern. AI produces a complete first draft; the human edits toward final quality. The blank page problem is eliminated. The human's role shifts from construction to curation and judgment.

Best for
  • Correspondence and client communications
  • Memos and internal documents
  • Reports, summaries, and executive briefings
  • Presentations and talking points
Human role
  • Verify all factual claims
  • Adjust tone and relationship context
  • Add judgment and nuance AI can't supply
  • Final approval and accountability
💡
Time savings estimate

Studies on legal drafting have found 40–70% time reductions on first-draft tasks. McKinsey estimates ~30% productivity gain for knowledge workers adopting AI in drafting workflows. Gains are highest for routine correspondence and lower for highly specialized documents requiring deep domain judgment.

Analyze → Synthesize

Feed large documents, data sets, or multiple sources to the model and ask it to surface patterns, inconsistencies, or key points. The model's ability to hold large contexts in memory and process them quickly creates genuine leverage for research-heavy work.

Best for
  • Contract review and issue-spotting
  • Multi-document research synthesis
  • Due diligence document review
  • Meeting note summarization
Cautions
  • Verify all extracted facts independently
  • Model may miss subtle or implied issues
  • Confident tone ≠ accurate extraction
  • Chunk large documents; don't rely on distant recall

Brainstorm → Filter

Use AI to generate a wide set of options, arguments, risks, or alternatives — then apply your judgment to filter, rank, and select. The model is fast and unconstrained in generation; you bring domain knowledge, stakeholder context, and judgment that the model lacks.

Best for
  • Risk identification and scenario planning
  • Generating counterarguments
  • Policy options and alternatives analysis
  • Agenda and presentation structure
Power prompts
  • "Generate 10 counterarguments to this position"
  • "What are 8 risks I may be underweighting?"
  • "What would a skeptical reviewer object to?"
  • "What am I not considering?"

Transform → Verify

AI excels at reformatting, restructuring, translating, or converting content between formats. Turning bullet points into prose, formal documents into plain-language summaries, or meeting notes into action item lists are high-reliability AI tasks with low hallucination risk — the source material is provided and the output is easily verified.

Best for
  • Plain-language summaries for clients
  • Converting dense analysis to slide decks
  • Reformatting data and tables
  • Tone adjustment (formal↔informal)
Why it's reliable
  • All facts are grounded in the input
  • You can verify output against source
  • Model isn't generating new claims
  • Low stakes for errors — easy to catch

Research → Report

Provide the model with source documents and ask it to synthesize a structured report on a specific question. This is not the same as asking the model to research from memory — grounding it in provided documents dramatically increases reliability and reduces hallucination risk.

Best for
  • Due diligence from provided filings
  • Policy memo from cited sources
  • Comparative analysis across documents
  • Summarizing regulatory guidance
Key principle

Grounded synthesis (from provided docs) is far more reliable than ungrounded synthesis (from model memory). Always anchor important research tasks to documents you control.

Build → Deploy

AI can write, debug, and explain code with impressive reliability for standard tasks. Even non-developers can use AI to build useful tools — data processing scripts, automations, spreadsheet formulas, and simple applications — without needing to understand the underlying code deeply.

Best for
  • Python/Excel data processing scripts
  • Automation workflows
  • Regex patterns and formulas
  • Simple web tools and dashboards
Cautions
  • Test all code before relying on it
  • Verify logic, not just syntax
  • Be explicit about security requirements
  • Large codebases degrade in quality

Stacking workflows: the compound effect

The highest-leverage users chain multiple patterns in sequence. A single complex task might move through: Brainstorm → Filter (identify the right angle) → Research → Report (ground the analysis) → Draft → Refine (produce the deliverable) → Transform → Verify (adapt for different audiences).

Example: preparing a client memo

① Ask AI to generate risks and considerations for the topic (Brainstorm). ② Feed relevant documents and ask for a synthesis of key points (Research→Report). ③ Request a full memo draft incorporating the findings (Draft). ④ Iterate on tone, length, and structure (Refine). ⑤ Request a plain-language version for the client (Transform). Each step takes minutes rather than the hours a purely manual approach would require.

Research power prompts

Synthesis

"I am attaching [N] documents. Based only on these documents, provide a structured summary of: (1) the key arguments, (2) areas of agreement across sources, (3) areas of disagreement, and (4) gaps or open questions not addressed."

Issue spotting

"Review the attached document and identify any statements, claims, or data points that appear to require additional verification, sourcing, or are inconsistent with standard practice in [DOMAIN]. Flag each with a brief explanation."

Comparative

"Compare the following two [documents/positions/approaches] across these dimensions: [X, Y, Z]. Present as a structured table with a brief narrative summary of the most significant differences."

Scenario planning

"Given the following situation, identify: (1) the three most likely outcomes, (2) the two tail risks I should plan for, and (3) the key variables that most affect which outcome occurs."

Reliability

Moderate to high for identifying obvious issues; lower for subtle, context-dependent, or jurisdictionally specific concerns. Best as a first pass, not a final review.

Critical caution

Never rely on AI document review as a substitute for qualified legal or professional review. AI can surface issues for human investigation — not resolve them.

Document review workflow

  1. 1
    Extract the relevant section

    Don't paste the whole document. Identify the clause, section, or provision you want analyzed and provide that specific text.

  2. 2
    Set context and scope

    Tell the model the document type, governing law, and what type of issues to look for (ambiguity, missing definitions, inconsistency, market standards).

  3. 3
    Specify output format

    Request numbered issues with severity flags and a brief explanation. This makes the output actionable and easy to triage.

  4. 4
    Human review of flagged items

    Treat AI output as a checklist of items for human investigation — not conclusions. Each flagged item requires professional judgment to evaluate.

Reliability

Very high. Meeting support tasks (summarization, action item extraction, agenda creation) are low-hallucination applications with provided content.

Time savings

Meeting preparation and follow-up are often the most time-intensive administrative work for knowledge workers. AI can cut this substantially.

Before meetings

  • Generate discussion questions
  • Anticipate objections or concerns
After meetings
  • Extract action items from raw notes

Underused application

AI as a personal tutor is one of the highest-leverage but least-used applications. On-demand explanation, worked examples, and Socratic dialogue accelerate learning dramatically.

Best technique

Ask it to explain something, then push back, ask for a different angle, request a worked example, ask it to quiz you. Conversation is more effective than passive reading.

Learning prompt patterns

Explain + Analogy

"Explain [CONCEPT] to me as if I'm an expert in [MY DOMAIN] but unfamiliar with this specific topic. Use an analogy to [FAMILIAR CONCEPT]."

Socratic probe

"Ask me questions about [TOPIC] to help me identify gaps in my understanding. Don't give me answers yet — let me attempt each one first."

Worked example

"Walk me through a realistic worked example of [CONCEPT] with real numbers and a plausible scenario. Show your reasoning at each step."

Pre-mortem

"If someone misunderstood [CONCEPT], what are the 3 most common errors they'd make? How would you spot those errors in practice?"

Agentic AI

The next frontier is not just AI that responds to prompts — it's AI that takes actions in the world. Agentic AI systems can use tools, browse the web, write and execute code, send emails, manage files, and complete multi-step tasks autonomously. This represents a fundamentally different mode of AI use, with dramatically higher leverage and proportionally higher risk.

What makes AI "agentic"

🛠️
Tool use

The model can call external tools: run code, search the web, read/write files, call APIs, send messages. Actions affect the real world, not just the chat window.

🔁
Multi-step reasoning

The model can break a goal into sub-tasks, execute them in sequence, incorporate results from earlier steps, and adapt its plan based on what it finds.

🤖
Reduced human-in-loop

Unlike chat, agentic workflows may run for minutes or hours, making many decisions autonomously before surfacing a result for human review.

Agentic capabilities available today

CapabilityWhat it enablesMaturity
Code execution AI writes and runs code in sandboxed environments — data analysis, file processing, calculations Production
Web search & browsing Real-time research and information retrieval beyond training cutoff Production
File creation & editing AI creates, reads, and modifies documents, spreadsheets, and other files Production
Tool & API integration AI calls calendar, email, CRM, and other systems via APIs on your behalf Maturing
Computer use AI operates a computer interface — clicks, types, navigates GUIs autonomously Early
Multi-agent orchestration Multiple AI agents collaborate in parallel on complex, decomposable tasks Early

The oversight imperative

As AI systems take more autonomous action, the oversight framework must scale accordingly. The same capability that makes agentic AI valuable — its ability to act without constant direction — makes unchecked errors harder to catch and more impactful.

Minimal footprint principle

Well-designed agentic systems request only the permissions they need, take the most reversible action available at each step, and pause to confirm before irreversible actions. Design your agentic workflows this way.

Irreversibility risk

An AI that sends an email, deletes a file, or posts publicly cannot undo that action. The risk profile of agentic AI is fundamentally different from chat AI — errors propagate into the real world.

⚖️
For professional contexts

Any agentic workflow that touches client data, financial transactions, legal filings, or regulated communications requires careful policy design, audit trails, and human approval checkpoints — not because AI can't do the task, but because the accountability framework in regulated industries demands it. Build the oversight in from the start.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

RAG is the most important technique for making AI reliable on specific, current, or proprietary knowledge. Rather than relying on the model's training data, RAG retrieves relevant documents from a database and injects them into the context window — so the model is reasoning from your actual documents, not its own memory.

RAG architecture — what the model receives
System
You are an assistant with access to the firm's document library. Answer questions only from the provided context. If the answer is not in the context, say so.
Retrieved Context [auto-injected]
[EXCERPT FROM DOCUMENT 1 — matched by semantic search] [EXCERPT FROM DOCUMENT 2 — matched by semantic search] [EXCERPT FROM DOCUMENT 3 — matched by semantic search]
User query
What are the distribution standards in the Henderson Family Trust?
Model response
Based on the provided document excerpts, the Henderson Family Trust distribution standards require the Trustee to consider... [grounded answer from retrieved documents]

RAG dramatically reduces hallucination because the model is citing provided text, not generating from memory. Building a RAG system over your firm's document library is one of the highest-ROI AI infrastructure investments available.

The hallucination taxonomy

Type 1
Factual fabrication

The model invents specific facts — case citations, regulatory section numbers, executive names — with confident, fluent prose. These are hardest to catch because they look authoritative.

Type 2
Outdated information

The model's training has a cutoff date. Regulatory changes, market developments, and legal updates after that date may not be reflected. The model often doesn't know what it doesn't know.

Type 3
Context drift

In long conversations, the model can subtly shift its understanding of your situation or constraints, producing responses that no longer match what you actually specified early in the conversation.

Type 4
Confident uncertainty

The model may not adequately signal when it's uncertain. Its calibration on expressing doubt is imperfect — a confidently-stated answer may reflect low reliability that isn't communicated in the text.

Data and confidentiality

Before pasting any client data, confidential documents, or proprietary information into a consumer AI product, understand how that data is handled.

Questions to ask
  • Is my input data used to train future models?
  • Is conversation content stored, and for how long?
  • What are the enterprise/API data handling terms?
  • Does my firm's data governance policy address AI?
Practical safeguards
  • Use enterprise/API tiers with stricter data commitments
  • Redact client-identifying information where possible
  • Understand your firm's AI use policy before proceeding
  • Prefer local or private-deploy options for sensitive work
🔒
Fiduciary context

In financial services and legal work, inputting client information into consumer AI products may implicate confidentiality obligations, fiduciary duties, and firm data governance policies. Treat AI data handling with the same rigor as any third-party service handling client information.

Building good verification habits

  1. 1
    Never trust specific facts without checking

    Names, dates, case citations, regulatory numbers, statistical claims — verify these against primary sources before using them in any professional context.

  2. 2
    Read the output, don't skim it

    The fluency of AI-generated text creates a false sense of familiarity. Actually read what was generated. Errors often appear mid-paragraph where attention drops.

  3. 3
    Ask for uncertainty

    Explicitly ask: "Where are you least confident in this analysis?" or "What am I assuming here that might not be true?" Models can surface uncertainty when asked, even if they don't volunteer it.

  4. 4
    Use AI for drafts, human judgment for decisions

    The clearest principle: use AI to accelerate production, not to replace judgment. The professional remains responsible for the decision, the filing, the advice — regardless of how much AI assisted in producing it.

Glossary

Key terms for navigating AI in professional practice.

Prompting & interaction

PromptThe full input provided to an AI model. Includes system instructions, context, examples, and the user message. Prompt design is the primary lever for controlling output quality.
System promptInstructions provided to the model before the user message — typically defining persona, scope, tone, and behavioral rules. Usually invisible to end users; set by developers or operators.
Few-shot promptingIncluding 2–3 examples of the desired input→output pattern in the prompt. The most reliable way to specify format and style. Named for the few examples provided (vs. zero-shot, which uses none).
Chain-of-thoughtA prompting technique that asks the model to show its reasoning step by step before delivering an answer. Substantially improves accuracy on multi-step logical or mathematical problems.
Context windowThe maximum amount of text the model can process in one call. Everything within it can be referenced; anything outside it is not available. Current frontier models support 128K–1M tokens.
TemperatureA parameter controlling output randomness. Low temperature produces more deterministic, conservative responses. High temperature produces more varied, creative outputs. Typical range: 0–1 for production use.

Reliability & quality

HallucinationWhen a model generates confident, fluent, but factually incorrect output. A structural consequence of how LLMs generate text — not a bug that will be fully eliminated. Verification is essential.
GroundingAnchoring model output to specific, provided source material rather than model memory. Grounded generation is substantially more reliable than ungrounded. Always provide documents for fact-sensitive tasks.
RAGRetrieval-Augmented Generation. A system that retrieves relevant documents and injects them into the context window before generation. Enables AI to answer questions from proprietary or current document libraries reliably.
Training cutoffThe date after which no information was included in the model's training data. Knowledge of events, regulations, or developments after this date is unavailable without web search or document injection.
CalibrationHow well a model's expressed confidence matches its actual accuracy. A well-calibrated model says "I'm uncertain" when it actually is uncertain. LLMs are imperfectly calibrated — confident-sounding outputs may be wrong.

Systems & architecture

Agentic AIAn AI system that uses tools and takes autonomous actions — browsing the web, writing and running code, sending messages, managing files — to complete multi-step tasks with reduced human involvement in each step.
Tool useThe ability of an LLM to call external functions or APIs — search tools, code executors, file systems, calendar APIs — and incorporate results into its response. The mechanism behind agentic AI capabilities.
Multi-agentAn architecture where multiple AI agents collaborate on a task — one decomposing the problem, others executing sub-tasks in parallel, one synthesizing results. Enables parallelization of complex workflows.
APIApplication Programming Interface. In AI context: the programmatic interface for sending prompts and receiving responses from a model, typically with more configurability, enterprise data handling, and integration options than a consumer interface.
Fine-tuningAdapting a pre-trained model for specific tasks or behaviors using additional training on curated data. Produces models with specialized capability — a model fine-tuned on legal documents will perform better on legal tasks than a general model.

Professional practice

Force multiplierA tool or technique that amplifies the output of a skilled practitioner without replacing their judgment. AI is a force multiplier — it increases the throughput of skilled knowledge workers, not a substitute for their expertise.
Human-in-the-loopA workflow design where a human reviews or approves AI outputs before they take effect. The appropriate oversight level depends on reversibility, stakes, and reliability of the AI task in question.
Prompt engineeringThe practice of designing, structuring, and refining prompts to elicit high-quality AI outputs consistently. A professional skill — not a technical one — that directly determines how much value you get from AI tools.
Minimal footprintA design principle for agentic AI: request only necessary permissions, prefer reversible actions, and pause to confirm before irreversible steps. Reduces risk of compounding errors in autonomous workflows.
